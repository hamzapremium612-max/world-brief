Pakistan's PTI-state standoff sharpens ahead of the long march, as fuel prices climb again

The state is squeezing PTI ahead of its planned long march while households face another fuel price hike, showing pressure building on more than one front at once.

Pakistan's Election Commission again barred three former lawmakers — Zartaj Gul, Sahibzada Hamid Raza and Abdul Latif — from contesting elections, for failing to file asset and liability statements. In Peshawar, PTI's interim chairman Gohar Ali Khan warned against rushing into creating new provinces without consensus, saying it could harm the federation. Goods transporters demanded that Islamabad police release containers seized ahead of PTI's planned long march to the capital later this month. On the same day, the government raised petrol and diesel prices again, with petrol now above Rs367 and diesel near Rs393 per litre.

Iran's nuclear standoff drags oil markets higher

An active US-Iran conflict and a fresh UN referral are pushing oil prices up worldwide, and Pakistan is positioning itself as a mediator at the UN.

The IAEA's 35-nation board voted to report Iran to the UN Security Council over its nuclear non-compliance, a move Iran, Russia and China rejected; Tehran says US and Israeli strikes on its nuclear sites have disrupted inspections. Brent crude hit $100 a barrel for the first time since July after the US struck five Iranian tankers. Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif will travel to New York for the UN General Assembly later this month and is set to speak on the 25th, highlighting Pakistan's efforts to end the US-Iran war. The session will also decide whether to renew the panel monitoring Iran sanctions, whose tenure expires while the assembly is in session.

India's mosque demolition reignites fears of communal politics

A mosque demolition read as electoral strategy raises the stakes for India's Muslim minority and for tension ahead of a major state election.

India's Modi government is facing criticism from politicians and activists after the demolition of a 115-year-old mosque in Saharanpur, Uttar Pradesh. Critics say the demolition is an attempt to communalise politics in the state ahead of its 2027 assembly elections. The criticism has come from a range of voices, from opposition politicians to civil society activists.

Pakistan's diplomacy shifts, as global pressure mounts on Israel

Pakistan is reshuffling its top diplomatic leadership just as international pressure, from Western governments to global brands, builds against Israel's settlement policy.

Pakistan's Deputy Prime Minister and Foreign Minister Ishaq Dar named Asim Iftikhar Ahmad, currently Pakistan's UN envoy, as the country's new foreign secretary. Separately, Pakistan joined seven other Arab and Muslim nations in welcoming the UK's new ban on imports from Israeli settlements in the occupied West Bank, a measure the UK imposed alongside Canada and France. Pakistani officials said they hope the move will help mobilise other countries toward similar action. The same kind of pressure is showing up commercially: Adidas faced global boycott calls after a campaign featuring a former Israeli soldier, prompting Adidas Arabia to apologise.

North Korea's nuclear buildup meets a rare warm gesture from Islamabad

Pakistan's public warmth toward Pyongyang lands the same week international inspectors flag North Korea's expanding nuclear infrastructure, a juxtaposition with real resonance given Pakistan's own nuclear history.

President Asif Ali Zardari and Pakistan's Foreign Office made an unusually public diplomatic gesture toward North Korea, sending greetings on the 78th anniversary of its founding and referring to "cordial relations" between the two countries. The same week, the International Atomic Energy Agency said North Korea has built a new two-storey uranium enrichment facility, calling it a cause for "serious concern." The two stories landed side by side in the news, putting Pakistan's warm message and international alarm over North Korea's nuclear program in the same frame.

Trump's assertive, personalized use of American power

This week shows the character of American power under Trump: unilateral force abroad, personal patronage at home, and a trade war with an ally, all centred on one man.

US Secretary of State Marco Rubio, visiting Ecuador, said Washington will work with allied countries against drug trafficking but insisted the US military will keep striking "narco-terrorists" unilaterally; the latest boat strike killed three people. The Trump administration also designated Ecuador's Los Tiguerones gang a terrorist organisation and plans to seek extra funding from Congress to dismantle it. At home, public records showed Trump gave $45,000 in cash gifts to aide Natalie Harp and two others, which the White House called personal and legal. Republicans opened a midterm convention in Dallas built around Trump, betting his presence can rescue the party despite his negative approval ratings, while the US also moved to ban imports of some Canadian alcohol, dairy and motorbikes in its ongoing trade war with Canada.
