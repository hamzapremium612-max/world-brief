Pakistan's economic stabilisation push

It shows Pakistan buying credibility with international lenders while passing the cost of that stabilisation onto its own citizens.

Pakistan raised a record $3 billion through a dual-tranche Eurobond sale that drew nearly $6bn in orders, and the Asian Development Bank's vice president praised the country's macroeconomic stabilisation and its shift toward private-sector-led growth. The Punjab Revenue Authority collected Rs54.8 billion in its first two months, and lenders more broadly cited fiscal and external improvements as reasons for continued support. China separately donated livestock vaccines under CPEC 2.0, a small sign that the China relationship continues alongside this outreach to Western capital markets. But the government also raised petrol and diesel prices this week, with taxes and duties making up a large share of the new pump price, a reminder that the stabilisation being praised abroad is being funded at home.

Pakistan's rule of law and accountability at home

It tests whether accountability in Pakistan reaches evenly across hospitals, the military's own institutions, a former prime minister's family, and a religious minority.

A fire in the gynaecology ward of Islamabad's Pakistan Institute of Medical Sciences prompted Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif to convene an emergency meeting and vow no leniency for officials found negligent, directing the health minister and secretary to oversee safety improvements. Separately, the Islamabad High Court set aside a 2022 verdict that had declared the Pakistan Naval Farms and Navy Sailing Club illegal, cancelling the earlier order to demolish them. Dr Uzma filed a second contempt petition, naming the prime minister and four other officials, over the continued denial of Imran Khan's family visits and phone calls with his sons. And in Punjab, a member of the Ahmadi community was shot dead in a suspected targeted killing that police are still investigating. Taken together, accountability is moving in different directions across these four stories at once.

Afghanistan, India and Pakistan's unsettled neighbourhood

Pakistan's two hardest bilateral relationships, with Afghanistan and with India, remain stuck on the same unresolved disputes over militancy and water.

Pakistan's Foreign Office said there will be no thaw in relations with Kabul until Afghanistan takes concrete, verifiable action against militant groups including the TTP and BLA, even while acknowledging some diplomatic progress had been made. The outgoing spokesperson also pressed India to implement the Indus Waters Treaty, saying New Delhi cannot treat international law as a matter of convenience. Separately, Pakistan marked the death anniversary of Kashmiri leader Syed Ali Geelani, with tributes describing his struggle as continuing to inspire advocates of a peaceful resolution to the Kashmir dispute. Both stories point to the same underlying condition: normalisation with Pakistan's neighbours keeps being made conditional on commitments that have not been fully met.

The Iran war's fallout

The war is now producing a civilian-casualty controversy, falling US public support, and visible strain on deployed US forces all at once, with no sign of quick resolution.

Iran says a US strike killed four people, including two children, at a wedding, calling it a war crime, which Washington denies; Iran responded by launching missiles and drones at US targets in the Middle East. Saudi Arabia said an Iranian attack on the tanker Sidr in the Strait of Hormuz killed two Filipino sailors. American public opinion has turned further against the war, with a new poll putting opposition at 54% against 39% support, the highest opposition level in six months. The strain is showing on deployed US forces too: the aircraft carrier USS Abraham Lincoln has finally reached port in Thailand after roughly nine months at sea, with reports of deteriorating living conditions and mental health problems among its crew. One unexpected side effect has been a dip in China's carbon emissions, as the war disrupted oil markets and pushed faster uptake of electric vehicles.

Russia's pressure campaign on Europe

Russia is testing Europe's resolve through drone incidents and veiled threats while European states quietly hedge against the risk of wider conflict.

Germany blamed Russia for a failed drone attack on Leipzig airport, and the EU and Nato responded by vowing to step up pressure on Moscow, which they describe as growing increasingly reckless. Ukraine's President Zelensky urged airlines to avoid Russian airspace as Ukraine escalates its own drone operations, while Russia said it would intensify its attacks on Ukraine in turn. The BBC's Steve Rosenberg described Putin's veiled threat toward the UK as part of a wider Russian campaign to keep the West guessing about its intentions. Separately, the Netherlands moved billions in gold reserves from the US and Canada to London, explicitly citing 'increasing geopolitical unrest' as the reason.

Trump's personalised, coercive foreign policy

Trump is using symbolic gestures and economic pressure, not just diplomacy, against both rivals and close allies like Canada.

Apple complied with a Trump executive order and renamed Lake Ontario 'Lake America' for US users of its Maps app, part of Trump's ongoing trade dispute with Canada. Trump also suggested renaming the Strait of Hormuz 'Trump Strait' on Truth Social, declaring the contested waterway under US control even as the war with Iran continues. His energy secretary, Chris Wright, defended the administration's oil deal with Venezuela against criticism that it amounts to 'gunpoint diplomacy', denying the US is seeking to seize the country's oil. And Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth faced backlash for a social media post body-shaming young female cadets in a Canadian armed forces programme, a post that stayed online despite anger on both sides of the border.
