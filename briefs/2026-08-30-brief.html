The Indus treaty holds, and a return through Wagah

The Indus Waters Treaty is the last working mechanism between two nuclear-armed rivals, so whether Pakistan can keep it insulated from politics matters more than any single statement from either capital.

At a Washington gathering on the Indus Waters Treaty, experts including Munir Akram and Sherry Rehman argued that Pakistan should not let political and security disputes become a reason to walk away from the treaty, with participant Rizwan Saeed describing it as facing a 'crisis of intent.' Rehman added that India cannot unilaterally suspend or reinterpret the agreement. Separately, 25 Pakistanis held in Indian jails were repatriated through the Wagah border crossing, with the returnees described as emotional on crossing back into Pakistan. Both stories point the same direction: the relationship is strained, but the channels built to manage it - the treaty, the border crossing - are still functioning.

Azad Kashmir's fragile politics

Azad Kashmir's government is juggling a party revolt and the fallout of a shutdown at the same time it depends on Islamabad's ruling family for buses - a small but telling picture of how limited its actual autonomy is.

Azad Kashmir's Prime Minister, Barrister Iftikhar Gillani, promised the business community and students that internet services at educational institutions and road links with Punjab and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa - both apparently restricted for some time - would be restored within days. Gillani also met Shah Ghulam Qadir to ease tension after the party favoured Gillani, a junior leader, for a nomination over Qadir, who has seniority and is reported to be unhappy about it. Separately, Gillani thanked Nawaz Sharif and Maryam Nawaz for providing green buses for Muzaffarabad and reviewed proposed routes and terminal facilities. The three stories together show a government handling internal party friction and basic service restoration while depending on Islamabad's ruling family for infrastructure.

Pakistan's energy squeeze

Four unrelated-looking stories - EVs, fuel prices, loadshedding and a dam - are all the same underlying problem: Pakistan cannot generate or import enough energy affordably, and every attempted fix runs into that limit.

The government's target of shifting 30% of vehicles to electric is stalling because oil marketing companies are expanding fuel outlets without adding chargers, even as petrol demand stays strong on the back of two-wheeler use; officials are meanwhile weighing a refinery upgrade costing an estimated $6 billion to cut the fuel import bill. Under the new daily pricing mechanism, petrol has risen by Rs25.87 and diesel by Rs17.09 since it began, meaning the increases have outweighed the cuts on net. The government separately apologised for night-time loadshedding caused by a shortage of RLNG cargoes, promising relief once the delayed shipments arrive. WAPDA also had to publicly deny that the power-generation component of the Diamer Bhasha Dam was being scrapped, saying the Rs1.424 trillion project remains approved with civil works due to start by June 2027.

PIMS fire fallout and Pakistan's accountability gap

Two fatal fires, two outcomes - criminal charges and a medal in one case, an unresolved 78-page report in the other - and the gap between them says more about how Pakistan assigns blame than either fire does on its own.

After a fire at the Pakistan Institute of Medical Sciences (Pims) killed 14 infants, Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif ordered the suspension of eight officials, including the hospital's executive director, and directed that criminal proceedings begin against them, based on an inquiry panel's interim report. He also announced a Rs10 million award and the Sitara-e-Khidmat honour for a nurse described as having acted bravely during the fire. In a separate case, a 78-page judicial commission report into the Gul Plaza fire blamed multiple factors but left responsibility for the blaze unresolved. The difference between a fast, named response to the Pims fire and an inconclusive report on Gul Plaza shows how much accountability after a disaster in Pakistan depends on which institution is at fault and who is watching.

Imran Khan and the information war

The government is now arguing with the international press rather than with Khan's party directly, which says something about how far the story has already travelled beyond Pakistan's borders.

Pakistan's Ministry of Information and Broadcasting rejected what it called 'misleading stories' from foreign media about a 'convicted prisoner' held in the country, after CNN anchor Becky Anderson interviewed former PTI aide Zulfi Bukhari and posted the ministry's statement online. In a separate statement, the ministry urged foreign media to reflect 'documented facts' in reporting on Imran Khan, rejecting reports of his isolation or medical neglect in jail. Both statements show a government treating international coverage of Khan's imprisonment as something requiring formal, repeated rebuttal rather than silence.

Nepal-Tibet floods: disaster deepens in the Himalayas

This is the deadliest disaster currently unfolding in South Asia, and Nepal's request for outside help with identification and rescue shows a state whose own capacity has been overwhelmed.

The number of people missing after floods in Nepal and Tibet has risen to nearly 3,000, with bad weather continuing to hamper search efforts. Nepal has asked other countries for help identifying and storing bodies, running DNA testing, and rescuing people still trapped in tunnels. Hospital walls in Nepal are reportedly covered with photographs of unidentified victims as authorities collect DNA samples and plan burials before morgues reach capacity, and 41 Australians remain among the missing. Families are described as balancing hope and despair as the search continues under worsening conditions.

SCO summit and Russia's war: the multipolar order under strain

Russia is trying to project strength through diplomacy - standing alongside China, Iran, Pakistan and India at a summit - at the same moment its war in Ukraine keeps producing the kind of civilian toll that undercuts that image.

Russia's Vladimir Putin and China's Xi Jinping are due in Kyrgyzstan for a two-day Shanghai Cooperation Organisation summit marking 25 years of the bloc, also attended by the leaders of Pakistan, Iran and India - a grouping that positions itself as a counterweight to Western influence. At the same time, a Russian drone strike hit a weapons depot near Kyiv, killing at least 37 people, damaging dozens of residential buildings including a care home, and prompting evacuations. Ukrainian prosecutors have opened an investigation into why the munitions were being stored so close to residential areas. The two stories sit side by side: Russia builds diplomatic standing through summitry even as its war continues to produce mass civilian casualties.
