Pakistan's political crisis: Imran Khan, the courts, and a fraying coalition

The fight over Imran Khan's medical treatment is now a full-blown contempt battle between the courts, the government and PTI, while the ruling coalition itself is struggling to get its own members into the National Assembly.

Pakistan's Supreme Court ordered on August 18 that PTI founder Imran Khan be moved to a private hospital for treatment, and the fallout is still working through Islamabad's institutions. The government and opposition traded contempt accusations in the Senate, PTI resubmitted a contempt petition on behalf of Imran's sister after the court raised objections to the first version, and PIMS hospital insisted its CT scanner is functional despite reports it failed to complete Imran's heart scan. Three other Adiala jail inmates have separately petitioned for private hospital care and greater family access, citing the same Supreme Court order. Meanwhile the ruling coalition is dealing with its own strain: National Assembly Speaker Ayaz Sadiq acknowledged repeated quorum failures, linked to a rift between PML-N and coalition partner PPP over the Azad Kashmir elections, though he says he expects the situation to improve.

Pakistan's regional diplomacy push

Army chief Asim Munir's Tehran visit, Washington's praise for his role, and Bangladesh's interest in a Pakistan-Saudi-Turkiye defence pact all point to Pakistan trying to expand its weight as a regional broker.

Pakistan's military and diplomatic leadership were visibly active across the region this week. Chief of Defence Forces Asim Munir met Iran's president in Tehran, with an Iranian official calling the visit's outcome a 'highly valuable diplomatic achievement' and dismissing media speculation about it, while a separate Dawn report on Iran's desire for the US to initiate de-escalation used that same Munir-Pezeshkian meeting as its backdrop. Washington's chargé d'affaires also praised Munir's role in regional peace and stability in a separate meeting with Pakistan's interior minister. Foreign Minister Ishaq Dar discussed bilateral ties and the upcoming Regional Four foreign ministers' meeting with his Turkish counterpart. And Bangladesh signalled interest in joining the Makkah Joint Defence Agreement between Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkiye, with a Bangladeshi official calling it unremarkable given Bangladesh's growing recognition among Muslim-world leaders.

Pakistan's energy security push

Islamabad is trying to move on two long-stuck energy problems at once - building a strategic oil reserve and reviving the stalled Iran gas pipeline - both central to Pakistan's chronic energy insecurity.

Pakistan is moving on two fronts to shore up its energy security. The Petroleum Division has launched a feasibility study on strategic petroleum reserves, awarding the work to Wood Mackenzie after a competitive tender, under Federal Minister Ali Pervaiz Malik. Separately, the prime minister has formed a high-level committee to try to move the stalled Pakistan-Iran gas pipeline out of arbitration, with the petroleum minister telling the Senate that both governments are working toward an amicable resolution.

India's rights record questioned at the UN

A UN watchdog's findings on India's treatment of tribal peoples, castes and Bengali-speaking Muslims put a formal international marker on rights issues that matter directly to how India is viewed in the region.

A UN human rights watchdog has rebuked India over its treatment of marginalised communities. The Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination said tribal peoples and disadvantaged castes in India face extrajudicial killings, torture and violence by law enforcement, and called on New Delhi to strengthen protections. The committee also said India urgently needs to address hate crimes against Bengali-speaking Muslims.

Iran-US sanctions dispute widens to China

Washington's push to isolate Tehran now directly threatens Beijing, Iran's biggest oil customer, turning a regional sanctions fight into a US-China friction point that touches the wider security picture around Pakistan's neighbourhood.

The US has escalated its sanctions campaign against Iran to explicitly threaten countries that keep trading with Tehran, and China, by far the largest buyer of Iranian oil, is the main target. Beijing called the new sanctions 'illegal' and said it would take 'all necessary measures' to protect its interests. The dispute is no longer just about Iran; it is now a direct US-China friction point over energy trade.

Trump asserts executive power at home and abroad

A Supreme Court win on voting rules, a reversal on Syria's terrorism designation, resumed lethal strikes, and a new tariff fight with Canada all show the same administration testing how far it can push, at home and internationally, in one week.

Several Trump administration moves converged this week. The Supreme Court's conservative majority sided with Trump on restricting mail-in voting ahead of the midterms, overturning a lower court injunction. Washington also removed Syria from its list of state sponsors of terrorism, formally embracing the country's new president, a former al-Qaeda-linked militant, and the US military resumed lethal strikes on suspected drug-trafficking vessels in the eastern Pacific after a months-long pause. On trade, Trump imposed a new 50% tariff on Canadian cars, trucks and steel, and Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney rejected a US proposal he said would weaken the French language, with neither side showing signs of backing down.
