The Hague rules against India on the Indus Waters Treaty

An international court has publicly rejected India's attempt to suspend a treaty it had already put in abeyance, reinforcing Pakistan's legal position on a resource dispute that touches its water security.

The Permanent Court of Arbitration ordered India to uphold the Indus Waters Treaty with Pakistan and to suspend construction on a hydropower project in Indian-administered Kashmir. The court said the treaty remains fully in force and that India has no justification for suspending it, rejecting the unilateral abeyance New Delhi declared in April last year. The ruling is a direct rebuke of India's position and reasserts the treaty's legal standing regardless of the broader political relationship between the two countries. It does not by itself force India to comply, but it strengthens Pakistan's hand in any future negotiation or dispute over the treaty.

Punjab's new anti-terrorism law and a tightening security state

A militant attack in Balochistan, a sharp cut in arms licensing, and a contested new law in Punjab are three pieces of the same story: the state expanding its security powers while insisting the threat justifies it.

The Punjab Assembly passed the Anti-Terrorism (Amendment) Bill, 2026, over a full walkout by opposition lawmakers, who call it a black law. Separately, the federal government has cut issuance of non-prohibited-bore arms licences by more than 90 percent, with a Senate committee told that 60 percent of those involved in terrorism incidents in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan were Afghan nationals. That crackdown comes as security forces killed 12 armed attackers who struck a convoy travelling to the Reko Diq copper and gold project in Chagai. Together, the three moves show a state responding to a live militant threat by widening its own legal and administrative reach, over the objections of an opposition that sees the new law as overreach rather than security policy.

Azad Kashmir: internet restored, but the unrest that caused it isn't

AJK's government is easing the symptom, a months-long internet blackout, while the underlying demand, elections in constituencies where voting was postponed, remains unmet.

The AJK government announced a phased restoration of internet services, with broadband and cellular data to resume fully in some districts while other areas keep restricted access limited to educational institutions and banks. The internet had been suspended in Rawalakot since June 5 amid protests organised by the Jammu Kashmir Joint Awami Action Committee (JAAC), and was only partially restored after 48 days. Even as the connectivity issue eases, peace marches took place in Palandri and Sudhnoti demanding an end to unrest and blockades and calling for immediate elections in seven constituencies of Poonch and Sudhnoti, where voting originally scheduled for July 27 was postponed. Political and religious leaders and civil society groups used the marches to call for peace and stability and for public rights to be protected.

Pakistan to lead the Makkah Defence Alliance secretariat

Pakistan is being handed formal leadership of a new Saudi-Turkish defence framework, extending its long-standing role as the Muslim world's preferred source of military leadership.

Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkiye agreed to further institutionalise their cooperation under the Makkah Agreement, with the alliance's secretariat to be headed by Pakistan for an initial three years, the Foreign Office said. The decision came at the first meeting of the pact's Strategic Political and Defence Committee, intended to strengthen collective deterrence and defence among the three states. Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan said the pact launches a process toward a new regional security and cooperation architecture. Pakistan's role as the secretariat's first head gives it an early institutional hand in shaping how that architecture develops.

Gulf strikes resume, and Pakistani capital comes home

Renewed US-Iran fighting is not just a Middle East story; it is already reshaping where Pakistani money is parked, reversing years of capital flowing out to Dubai property.

The United States and Iran traded strikes for the first time in weeks, with an attack on Larak Island in the Strait of Hormuz killing two people and injuring two others, the first known US strike since late July. Iran's president said the country still wants a negotiated end to the conflict, even as President Trump posted an AI-generated video clip claiming to show Iran's Kharg Island oil hub being destroyed. Separately, sources in Pakistan's property and currency markets say the Gulf war has curbed the outflow of black money from Pakistan to Dubai, with funds already invested there now returning and being redirected into property inside Pakistan. Pakistan had twice been identified as the second-largest source of foreign investment in Dubai property, so the reversal marks a real shift in where that capital sits.

Nepal and Tibet's flood disaster, and what China isn't saying

A transboundary flood disaster is exposing the same information gap that has long defined how Beijing handles bad news on the Tibetan plateau, at the same time as it threatens South Asian lives and infrastructure.

Rescuers in Nepal are using explosives to blast through a hillside in their search for hydropower workers, with the flooding death toll reaching 939. More than 900 workers are missing overall, with hundreds feared trapped in tunnels and underground areas across a dozen hydropower projects. Across the border, a Tibetan advocacy group says the flooding also destroyed hundreds of homes on the Tibetan side and that the true number of missing people there is far higher than Beijing has admitted. The border area near Gyirong Port, where dozens of missing Australians were last known to be, is described as almost entirely cut off and buried under metres of silt and debris, complicating any independent assessment of the damage.
