Nepal-Tibet glacial flood disaster

A glacial lake collapse on the Nepal-Tibet border has killed hundreds and left over a thousand missing, showing how a warming-driven Himalayan hazard can overwhelm two states' disaster response at once.

A glacial lake on the Nepal-Tibet border collapsed and triggered flash floods that have killed at least 469 people, according to Nepali police, with roughly 1,300 more still missing. Scientists say a collapsed glacier is the likely cause, pointing to the risks of rapidly melting Himalayan ice. China has halted rescue operations at the epicentre because a debris-formed lake is now overflowing toward the area rescue teams were heading into. Of those missing, 667 are tourists, including 540 foreign nationals from 31 countries, and entire villages have been swept away. Nepali and Tibetan diaspora communities abroad are organising vigils and fundraising for the recovery effort.

Pims fire tragedy and Pakistan's hospital safety reckoning

A newborn ward tragedy at Pakistan's top public hospital has forced a reckoning over fire safety across state-run health facilities, testing whether the response is a real fix or another paper directive.

Fourteen newborn babies died in a fire-related incident at the Pakistan Institute of Medical Sciences (Pims) in Islamabad, and the fallout is spreading. Pims has started referring serious gynaecology cases to the Polyclinic and hospitals in Rawalpindi while it sorts out its own wards, and Sindh's government has separately ordered a review of fire safety at all public buildings after health officials warned facilities remain vulnerable. A follow-up inspection by the Capital Development Authority found serious fire safety lapses at two more Islamabad hospitals, Capital Hospital and the Polyclinic. Politician Mustafa Kamal has said a judicial commission may be formed if the official Pims probe does not satisfy public concern, and that action will follow against whoever is found responsible.

Pakistan's dynastic and personality politics

Pakistan's two dominant political families are each managing succession and loyalty questions this week, a reminder that party politics here still runs through bloodlines and personal loyalty as much as institutions.

PML-N leader Hamza Shehbaz, the prime minister's son, has begun a more visible political role in Islamabad, meeting party lawmakers after keeping a low profile since his cousin Maryam Nawaz became Punjab's chief minister. The party has also picked a young leader, Barrister Syed Iftikhar Ali Gillani, as its candidate for Azad Kashmir's premiership, a decision PM Shehbaz Sharif made personally. Imran Khan's imprisonment continues to shape politics on the other side: his sons gave an interview raising concerns about his health, and the Islamabad High Court has reserved its verdict on petitions from other Adiala jail prisoners seeking the kind of facilities Khan has received, ruling that prisoners' rights cannot be dismissed just because they are behind bars. PTI has announced a rally in Karachi for September 6, led by Khyber Pakhtunkhwa's chief minister, saying it will go ahead even without official permission.

Pakistan's fuel economics under IMF constraints

Islamabad is trying to give households a small break on fuel prices just as the IMF is warning that doing the same on gas would blow up the circular debt its own programme is meant to fix.

The government trimmed petrol prices by Rs0.50 a litre and diesel by Rs0.19, though it still collects roughly Rs100-114 per litre in taxes and duties on both fuels. On the gas side, the IMF is opposing a similar price cut, warning it would increase Pakistan's circular debt. Jamaat-e-Islami is not satisfied either way: it has called a nationwide strike for September 3 against the petroleum levy, threatening to spread protests into markets and roads if the government does not offer more relief.

The specific numbers change each round, but the triangle of Fund, government and street has not.

Pakistan's regional security: Afghan threat, Gulf defence deals

Pakistan is managing a deteriorating security relationship with Afghanistan while deepening defence ties with Gulf states - two fronts of the same effort to shore up its western and Middle Eastern flanks.

Human Rights Watch has accused Afghanistan's Taliban government of a 'ruthless' crackdown after a new 84-page police law banned all protests nationwide. Pakistan's foreign minister, meeting Britain's foreign secretary, said he has 'serious concern' about terrorist threats reaching Pakistan from Afghan soil. On a separate front, Pakistan is expanding defence ties with the Gulf: its defence chief and a visiting Kuwaiti delegation pledged to build a 'mutually beneficial defence partnership', and the two countries signed a new protocol agreement under which Pakistan's armed forces will assist Kuwait. Bangladesh's foreign minister has also said Dhaka would 'positively' consider joining the Makkah pact if invited, suggesting the Islamic-bloc defence arrangement Pakistan belongs to is drawing wider interest.

Washington's assertive turn: Canada, Iran, Gaza

Washington is applying pressure on three different fronts at once - a neighbour, a sanctioned adversary, and a fragile Middle East ceasefire - a useful read on how this administration operates abroad.

Donald Trump signed an executive order renaming Lake Ontario as 'Lake America' after trade talks between the US and Canada collapsed; Canadian PM Mark Carney rejected the move, and several US state governors publicly mocked it too. In the Middle East, Qatar's prime minister travelled to Tehran to try to revive diplomatic channels as the US and Iran traded accusations over Washington's latest sanctions drive, which one Iranian official described as 'all-out economic war'. Separately, the Gaza envoy for the Board of Peace criticised both Israeli strikes and Hamas's actions, warning that a collapse of last October's ceasefire would be a 'point of no return' for the region.
